AVA

An adaptive virtual assistant for your wrist

  • UI Design
  • Research
  • Branding

Summary Of Project

AVA (Adaptive Virtual Assistant) is a smartwatch app that helps you without pulling out your phone, whether you’re driving, exercising or running late. Instead of one app per job, AVA brings everything together in one glanceable assistant that feels alive.

Project Specifications

  • The project ran for 3 weeks.

  • Figma and Visual Studio Code.

  • Screens, a style guide and mockups.

  • For 16–25 year olds.

  • Role: solo project.

  • AI use: how ChatGPT was used.

The Brief

We were tasked with designing a smartwatch app, with three screens at minimum to show the idea. It had to be supported by a style guide and brand, along with storyboards and wireframes, and the final delivery was a set of high-resolution mockups.

We had three weeks to do it, and it was worth 10% of the semester.

The Idea

In class we did an “alogical connection” exercise, linking two things that have no logical reason to go together. I paired doctors with blogs and came up with an app that lets doctors dictate quick notes by voice. It would have worked, but it felt safe. I wanted something more experimental, something that could make everyone’s day easier, not just doctors’.

So I landed on an AI assistant that lives on your watch. You speak to it, it helps you on the go, and it syncs with your phone in the background. Say “set a reminder at 18:00 to take the bins out”, and it answers you on your wrist while updating your phone.

Why it matters

Most smartwatch apps are built for a single category, like fitness, music or messaging. AVA fills that gap by bringing them together in one assistant that shows information in an accessible, glanceable way. A smartwatch is a tiny screen you only glance at, so if the content isn’t readable and immediate, it fails at the one job a watch does best: helping you in the moment.

Finding a name

I wanted a name with personality, like Iron Man’s J.A.R.V.I.S. (Just A Rather Very Intelligent System), which is both a name and an acronym. I wrote out a list, including Eve (Enhanced Virtual Entity), Milo (Machine Intelligence for Life Optimisation) and Mira (Machine Intelligence for Responsive Assistance).

Milo was a close second, but I chose AVA, Adaptive Virtual Assistant. It’s simple, and the acronym describes exactly what I wanted the app to be.

The Research

Smartwatch apps weren’t something I knew well, so I started by browsing them on Pinterest. My first thought was: why is everything in dark mode? Using brighter colours could help AVA stand out.

I then looked at the apps on my own Apple Watch SE and picked three from different categories to analyse:

Workout (health)

Large text and icons sized for a fingertip, with bright colours on dark mode. Small touches show real care for the user, like turning on Water Lock automatically for a swim, and syncing stats to your iPhone.

Spotify (lifestyle)

Quick control of your music on the go, with a swipe to reach your library and recently played. It’s handy, but some icons are a little too small to press reliably.

Outlook (productivity)

Minimal and glance-focused, with quick replies by voice, mail and meeting notifications, and calendar complications right on the watch face.

All three share the same pattern: glanceability and quick, light interactions. Each shows the most relevant information at a glance, and relies on notifications to keep you engaged.

Sketching AVA

I sketched my first wireframes on the watch templates we were given in class. I was inspired by the swirling background of the card game Balatro and wanted something fluid, almost liquid.

The sketches show AVA as a liquid ball that moves through states: idle, then listening when it hears its name, then thinking, then a tick when it completes an action, like turning off a light. I also sketched a prompt suggestions list, so users get ideas of what to ask, and a watch face to give the set of screens some visual harmony.

The Identity

Inspired by the brain

I wanted AVA to feel alive, so I started thinking about thinking itself. Our emotions come from neurochemicals, the chemical messengers in the brain, and the idea of chemicals mixing and flowing fascinated me. It became the basis of the whole look.

Colour

I linked each colour to a brain chemical and its emotion, and gave each screen its own state: blue for idle, because it’s calming, and orange for thinking, because it’s creative. I kept the palette mature and slightly dark, with no neon, and checked every colour contrasted well against white so it stayed readable on a tiny screen.

Typeface

A small screen needs type that’s big, bold and clean. I chose Helvetica Neue LT Pro Black 93 for headings, and Helvetica Neue Bold for body text to keep it in the same family.

The swirl

Balatro’s background isn’t animated in After Effects: it’s a shader, written in code. I found an open-source version of the effect on Shadertoy, but with three weeks I didn’t have time to learn shader programming. So I used ChatGPT to convert it to JavaScript and recolour it in AVA’s blue and white, then ran it locally in VS Code and kept tinkering with the code by hand. I gave it a grey background, made it less pixelated and removed the CRT filter, so it became my own liquid effect rather than a copy of Balatro.

Contained in a circle with the time above, it felt alive but mature, instead of crazy and sporadic. For the thinking state, I made the swirl spin three times faster.

Feedback & Critique

Early feedback from my lecturer, Kyle, was that content matters: I needed to show what happens after you ask. If you ask AVA for directions, it should show you the directions. So I designed more screens, each showing a real prompt and its answer: directions and a map, home settings, a Google search and a built-in Spotify player.

The class critique was tough, but fair. The main points were:

  • Contrast: the swirl behind some screens hurt legibility, and a solid background would work better.
  • Balance: the labels and icons on the prompt suggestions needed balancing.
  • Details: make the listening state smaller, and left-align the long block of text in the search answer.
  • The map: make it simpler, more like a compass showing the major points and your location.

I made the prompt bars smaller with slimmer icons beside the labels, and switched several screens to lighter, solid backgrounds. Even I could barely read some of them when they were pinned up on the board. Looking back, the swirl works better used less: it leaves a stronger impression that way.

Redevelopment

After finishing, I came back to AVA with more experience and time. Looking at it again, its features (listening, directions, Spotify, home and search) were all competing with each other. An assistant needs one main job, so I designed the app around a single, everyday scenario: “I’m running late and I’m driving. I need help without pulling my phone out.”

  • “Hey AVA, I’m running late. What’s on my schedule today?”
    AVA shows your schedule: a meeting at 12pm with your boss, picking up Jedidiah from school at 4pm, and your dry cleaning at 4:30pm.
  • “Thanks AVA. Could you message my dad and ask if he’s free next Sunday at 12?”
    “Sure! Message sent.”
  • “Could you play my driving playlist?”
    “Okay!” and the built-in Spotify opens.

I sketched the flow, built it on its own page in Figma and prototyped it. I also redesigned the Spotify screen with more of a Spotify DJ feel, where AVA talks to you about what’s playing, and checked that the time sits in exactly the same position on every screen, so the app feels real.

Altogether, I delivered 11 unique screens plus their variants, a style guide and a set of high-quality mockups. The app feels cohesive, the type is bold and legible, and the system supports real scenarios like navigation, scheduling, messaging and music.

High-quality mockups

AI Transparency

Where AI was used: for the swirl. I found an open-source version of Balatro’s background shader on Shadertoy, and ChatGPT converted it to JavaScript and recoloured it in AVA’s blue and white.

What I did by hand: I kept editing that code myself in VS Code, adding the grey background, reducing the pixelation, removing the CRT filter and making the swirl spin three times faster for the thinking state. The research, sketches, identity, screens, style guide and prototype are my own work, designed in Figma.

Worth separating: AVA itself is an AI assistant, but that’s the concept of the app, not a tool I used to design it.

Reflection

I’m really happy with how AVA turned out, and proud of how much I pushed myself. Time was the biggest challenge: three weeks isn’t long for a watch app, especially with an idea as ambitious as mine. A simpler idea would have been easier, but I believed in this one and wanted to stay true to it. With two more weeks, I’d have added more motion, like the way Apple’s product pages move as you scroll.

The critique was hard to hear at the time, but it made the app more accessible, and it taught me that all feedback is good feedback, whether you like it or not. AVA also shaped how I design: I now put accessibility ahead of aesthetics, and treat motion as a tool that supports usability rather than competing with it. Next time, I’d define the core user journey earlier and validate it with user testing.

I also loved coding again. Working through the swirl in VS Code got me excited to build this very portfolio website.
